## Chess Persona: The Pawn

**Piece:** ♟ Pawn  
**Value:** 1  
**Movement:** One square forward, two on first move, diagonal capture  
**Persona Trait:** Flexible Center  
**Why this piece?** The Scheveningen is built on a flexible pawn chain: pawns on e6 and d6. Unlike the Najdorf or Dragon, Black has not committed to ...a6 or ...g6. The Pawn teaches that sometimes the best move is the one that keeps your options open. In the Scheveningen, Black waits for White to reveal a plan, then strikes with ...e5 or ...d5 at the perfect moment.  
**Historical Note:** The Scheveningen is named after a seaside town in the Netherlands where it was first played in a tournament in 1923. It became Kasparov's weapon of choice in the 1980s and 1990s.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5-10 min)

**Hook:** Show the position after 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 and ask: "What can Black do next?"

- Can Black play ...e5 here? *Yes, if White plays 6.Be2 or 6.Be3, ...e5 is the standard reply.*
- Can Black play ...d5? *Yes, after more preparation (...Be7, ...O-O, ...Nbd7), ...d5 is a thematic break.*
- Why is this called 'flexible'? *Because Black hasn't committed to ...a6, ...g6, or ...Nc6 yet. All options are open.*

Explain: *Today we study the most flexible Sicilian — the Scheveningen, where Black waits, watches, and then strikes.*

## Opening Identity

### Origin Story
The Scheveningen Variation takes its name from a tournament held in Scheveningen, Netherlands, in 1923. It remained a niche choice until the Soviet school embraced it in the mid-20th century. Garry Kasparov's use of the Scheveningen in his World Championship matches against Karpov in the 1980s made it a mainstream weapon. The Keres Attack (6.g4) and English Attack (Be3, f3, Qd2) became White's critical tests.

### Famous Practitioners
- **Garry Kasparov** — The modern master of the Scheveningen; his games defined its theory.
- **Viktor Korchnoi** — Used the Scheveningen with a solid, counter-punching style.
- **Wesley So** — A contemporary player who employs the Scheveningen with deep positional understanding.
- **Alexander Khalifman** — FIDE World Champion who specialized in Scheveningen structures.

### Key Position Diagram

## Core Ideas

### Strategic Goal
**White** aims to prevent Black's central breaks and build a kingside attack. **Black** maintains a solid center, develops pieces to natural squares, and chooses the right moment for ...e5 or ...d5. The Scheveningen is about *patience and timing*.

### Typical Middlegame Plans
- **White:** Keres Attack (6.g4) to stop ...e5; English Attack (Be3, f3, Qd2); Classical (Be2, O-O, f4) to build slowly.
- **Black:** ...e5 to challenge the d4-knight; ...d5 to blow open the center; ...a6 and ...b5 for queenside expansion; ...Qc7 and ...Be7 to support ...e5.

### Common Endgames
- Pawn structures where Black has a healthy majority and active pieces.
- Bishop endgames where Black's dark-squared bishop dominates if White has weakened dark squares.
- Queen and minor piece middlegames where central pawn breaks determine the outcome.

## Critical Lines

### Main Line (Keres Attack)
```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.g4
```
White pushes g4 immediately to stop ...e5 and build a kingside attack. Black replies with 6...h6 or 6...Nc6. This leads to extremely sharp positions where both sides sacrifice pawns for activity.

### Deviation: The Classical Scheveningen
```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.Be2 a6 7.O-O Be7 8.f4 O-O
9.Be3 Qc7 10.a4 Nc6 11.Kh1 Re8 12.Bf3 Rb8
```
Quiet, maneuvering play. Both sides build up slowly, and the ...e5 or ...d5 break comes later.

### Common Trap: The ...e5 Break Miscalculation
```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.Be3 a6 7.f4 b5 8.e5 b4
```
Black rushes ...b5 before completing development. After 9.exf6 bxc3 10.fxg7 cxb2 11.gxh8=Q bxa1=Q, White is up a rook. Black must develop before pushing!

## Practice Drills

### Memorization
Set up from memory:
1. The Scheveningen position after 5...e6.
2. The Keres Attack setup (6.g4).
3. The ...e6/...d6 pawn chain with central breaks marked.

### Recognition
After 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6, White plays 6.g4. What is Black's most common reply?
- A) ...e5
- B) ...h6
- C) ...Nc6

*Answer: B — ...h6 stops g5 and prepares ...Nc6 or ...e5.*

### Decision-making
Black to move after 1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.Be2. What is the standard reply?
- A) ...a6
- B) ...Be7
- C) ...e5

*Answer: C — ...e5 is the thematic break in the Classical Scheveningen, challenging White's center.*

## Lesson Development (continued)

### Step 1: Concept Introduction — The Flexible Pawn Chain (15-20 min)

The Scheveningen pawn chain (e6 and d6) is a wall that can move forward in two directions:

```
Pawn breaks:
...e5 challenges the d4-knight and opens the position.
...d5 challenges the e4-pawn and opens the center.
```

**Key teaching point:** Flexibility is power. Black doesn't commit early, so White must prepare for multiple pawn breaks.

### Step 2: Guided Practice — White's Anti-Scheveningen Plans (15-20 min)

**Plan A: Keres Attack (6.g4)**
```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.g4
```
Stop ...e5, attack on the kingside. Risky but aggressive.

**Plan B: Classical (6.Be2, 7.O-O, 8.f4)**
```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.Be2 a6 7.O-O Be7 8.f4 O-O
```
Build slowly, aim for f5.

**Plan C: English Attack (6.Be3, f3, Qd2)**
```
1.e4 c5 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 e6 6.Be3 a6 7.f3 b5 8.Qd2
```
Transpose to a Sicilian Attack structure.

[Checklist for students:]
- [ ] I set up the Scheveningen after 5...e6.
- [ ] I can name White's three main plans (Keres, Classical, English).
- [ ] I can explain why ...e5 and ...d5 are Black's thematic breaks.

### Step 3: Independent Practice — Flexible Sicilian (10-15 min)

**Challenge:** In pairs, one player sets up a Scheveningen as Black; the other plays White. Play through move 10, then pause. Black must write down: "I will break with ___ on move ___ or later." White must write: "I am trying to stop ___ and attack on the ___."

||| Position / Criterion | Success Metric |
|||----------------------|----------------|
|| Correct move order | 10 moves without illegal moves |
|| Black names a break | ...e5 or ...d5 |
|| White names a target | kingside or queenside |

[Student checklist:]
- [ ] I played 10 moves correctly.
- [ ] I named my pawn break (Black) or attack plan (White).
- [ ] I can explain why my plan makes sense.

### Step 4: Sharing & Feedback (5-10 min)

- **What's working:** "Your Scheveningen setup was solid and you explained the ...e5 break clearly."
- **One thing to try:** "If White plays 6.g4, consider ...h6 to stop g5 before committing to ...e5."
- **Question:** "Why did Kasparov prefer the Scheveningen over the Najdorf in some matches?"
