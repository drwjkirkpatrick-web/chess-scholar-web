## Chess Persona: The Bishop

**Piece:** ♗ Bishop  
**Value:** 3 points  
**Movement:** Diagonally any number of squares  
**Persona Trait:** Solid Fianchetto  
**Why this piece?** The Queen's Indian is defined by the bishop on b7, fianchettoed quietly behind the ...b6 pawn. Like the Bishop sitting on a long diagonal, the Queen's Indian waits patiently, controlling the center from afar. It teaches us that pressure builds slowly but inevitably.  
**Historical Note:** The Queen's Indian became popular in the 1920s and 1930s as a solid alternative to the Nimzo-Indian. When White avoids 3.Nc3 (to prevent the ...Bb4 pin), Black can fianchetto the bishop on b7 instead. It has been a favorite of positional players like Capablanca and Petrosian.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5-10 min)

**Hook:** Show 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6. Ask: "Black has played ...b6, fianchettoing the bishop on b7. Is this too slow? Where is Black's counterplay?"

- *What does the bishop on b7 control?* — The e4-square and the long diagonal toward h1. It quietly prevents White from dominating the center.
- *How is this different from the Nimzo-Indian?* — In the Nimzo, Black plays ...Bb4 aggressively. In the Queen's Indian, Black develops solidly and waits.
- *Can White ignore the b7-bishop?* — No! That bishop controls e4, making it hard for White to build a big center.

Explain: *Today we'll study the Queen's Indian Defense using the Bishop as our guide — a quiet piece on a long diagonal that slowly dominates the game.*

### Step 1: Opening Identity — The Queen's Indian Story (15 min)

**Origin Story:**
The Queen's Indian emerged in the 1920s as a companion to the Nimzo-Indian. When White plays 3.Nf3 instead of 3.Nc3 (to avoid the Nimzo pin), Black has the option to fianchetto on b7. The opening was championed by hypermodern players who believed in controlling the center from the flank. It became especially popular after World War II, with Tigran Petrosian using it to build his fortress-like positions.

**Famous Practitioners:**
- **José Raúl Capablanca** — used the Queen's Indian to simplify positions toward winning endgames
- **Tigran Petrosian** — the "Iron Tigran" built impregnable positions with the Queen's Indian
- **Anatoly Karpov** — ground out countless wins with the Queen's Indian's quiet pressure
- **Ding Liren** — modern champion who trusts the Queen's Indian's solidity

**Key Position Diagram — The Queen's Indian Tabiya:**
After 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6 4.g3 Ba6 5.b3 Bb4+ 6.Bd2 Be7 7.Bg2 c6 8.O-O d5 9.Nbd2 Nbd7 10.Re1 c5, Black has the bishop outside on a6 (the "Queen's Indian bishop"), pressuring c4. The position is balanced but rich in maneuvering.

### Step 2: Core Ideas — Strategic Goals for Both Sides (15 min)

**Strategic Goal:**
- **Black:** Develop the bishop on b7 to control e4 and the long diagonal. Prepare ...d5 or ...c5 to challenge the center. Build a solid position and outmaneuver White in a quiet middlegame.
- **White:** Build a center with e4 or maintain control with d5. Keep the bishop pair. Use the extra space to create threats before Black's solid position becomes active.

**Typical Middlegame Plans:**
- **Black:** ...Ba6 (pressuring c4), ...d5 or ...c5 (central break), ...Ne4 (centralizing the knight), ...Rc8/...Qe7 (connecting rooks and preparing ...c5).
- **White:** e4 (if allowed), d5 (closing the center and playing on the wings), b3/c3 (supporting c4), Re1/Bf4 (active piece play).

**Common Endgames:**
- Queen's Indian endgames are famously favorable for Black if White overextends.
- Minor piece endgames with Black's b7-bishop controlling key squares are typical.
- Rook endgames with balanced pawns favor the better technician; Black's solid structure helps.

### Step 3: Critical Lines — Main Line, Deviation, and Trap (10 min)

**Main Line — The Petrosian Variation (4.a3):**
```
1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6 4.g3 Ba6 5.b3 Bb4+ 6.Bd2 Be7 7.Bg2 c6 8.O-O d5 9.Nbd2 Nbd7
```
White plays a3 to prevent ...Bb4 and prepares to expand in the center. Black continues with ...Ba6, ...c6, and ...d5.

**Deviation — The Old Main Line (4.e3):**
```
1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6 4.e3 Bb7 5.Bd3 Be7 6.O-O O-O 7.Nc3 d5 8.Qe2 c5
```
White plays quietly, allowing Black to complete development. This leads to classical, maneuvering battles.

**Common Trap — The c4 Trap:**
```
1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6 4.g3 Ba6 5.b3 Bb4+ 6.Bd2 Bxd2+ 7.Qxd2 Bb7 8.Bg2 d5 9.cxd5 Qxd5
10.Nc3 Qd7 11.O-O O-O 12.Rfd1 Qb7 13.Ne5 Rd8 14.Qe3 Nbd7 15.Bxb7 Qxb7 16.Nxd7 Rxd7 17.Rxd7 Nxd7
```
Black equalizes easily and White has nothing. Moral: the Queen's Indian is so solid that White must find active plans or risk drifting into a worse position.

### Step 5: Practice Drills (10 min)

**Drill 1 — Memorization:**
Set up the Queen's Indian position from memory after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6 4.g3 Ba6 5.b3 Bb4+ 6.Bd2 Be7.

**Drill 2 — Recognition:**
Show positions from the Queen's Indian, Nimzo-Indian, and Bogo-Indian. Ask students to identify the Queen's Indian by its ...b6/...Bb7 or ...Ba6 setup without ...Bb4.

**Drill 3 — Decision-making:**
After 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 b6, White can play 4.g3 (fianchetto), 4.e3 (solid), or 4.Nc3 (transposing to other systems). Which do you think gives White the most chances for an advantage?

### Step 6: Sharing & Feedback (5 min)

- **What's working:** "Your Queen's Indian setup with ...b6 and ...Bb7 is correctly placed."
- **One thing to try:** "Consider what happens if White plays 4.a3 — does your ...Bb4 plan still work?"
- **Question:** "Why do you think the Queen's Indian is less popular than the Nimzo at the very top level?"
