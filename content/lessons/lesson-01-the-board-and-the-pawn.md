# Lesson Plan 01: The Board & The Pawn

> *Meet the Pawn; learn the board, ranks, files, and algebraic notation.*

---

**Module Reference:** Prompt 01 — lesson-01-the-board-and-the-pawn.md  
**Duration:** 60 min  
**Age Group:** 8-10  
**Chess Skill Level:** ♙ Pawn Tier  
**Tool:** Physical board (primarily)

---

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. **Name** all 64 squares on a chessboard using algebraic notation.
2. **Move** a Pawn correctly — one square forward, two on first move, diagonal capture.
3. **Value** patience and foundation — understanding that every grandmaster started by learning the board one square at a time.

## Key Inquiry Question

> How does the Pawn move forward one step at a time — and how can we use the same patience to build our chess skills step by step?

## Chess Persona: The Pawn

**Piece:** Pawn  
**Value:** 1 point  
**Movement:** Forward one square (or two from starting rank), captures one square diagonally forward  
**Persona Trait:** Patience & Foundation  
**Why this piece?** The Pawn is the first piece every player learns, and its slow, steady movement teaches that small steps build into something powerful. Just as a house needs a foundation, a chess player needs patience. The Pawn’s journey from rank 2 to rank 8 mirrors a learner’s journey from beginner to master.  
**Historical Note:** The Pawn represents the infantry — foot soldiers — in medieval armies. In the oldest known form of chess, chaturanga from 6th-century India, the Pawn (called "padati," meaning foot soldier) was already the most numerous but weakest piece. It wasn't until the 15th century in Europe that Pawns gained the ability to move two squares on their first move, making the opening phase faster and more dynamic.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5-10 min)

**Hook:** Place a single White Pawn on e2. Ask students to describe where it is without pointing. Introduce the idea that every square has a "name."

- "If I told you to put a Pawn on the square where the letter 'e' meets the number '2,' could you do it?" *Yes — that is square e2.*
- "Why do you think the board uses letters and numbers instead of just colors?" *Because colors repeat; letters and numbers give every square a unique name.*
- "How many total squares are on the board?" *64 — 8 files × 8 ranks.*

Explain: *Today we'll learn the board and meet the Pawn, the most patient piece in chess. The Pawn will be our guide.*

### Step 1: Concept Introduction — The Board & Algebraic Notation (15-20 min)

**Teach the board layout:**
- Files are the **vertical columns** labeled a through h from White's left to right.
- Ranks are the **horizontal rows** labeled 1 through 8 from White's side to Black's side.
- Every square is named by its file letter + rank number (e.g., a1, h8, d4).
- The **light-colored** square is always on the **right corner** for each player ("Light on Right").

**Set up a position to practice naming squares:**

```
White pieces on their starting squares: Ra1, Nb1, Bc1, Qd1, Ke1, Bf1, Ng1, Rh1
White Pawns: a2, b2, c2, d2, e2, f2, g2, h2
```

Ask students to name the square under the White Queen. *d1.* Ask them to name the corner square near White's left hand. *a1.*

**Introduce the Pawn's movement:**
- From its starting square (rank 2 for White, rank 7 for Black), a Pawn may move **one or two squares forward**.
- On every later turn, a Pawn moves **exactly one square forward**.
- A Pawn **captures diagonally forward** one square — never straight ahead.

**Demonstration position:**

```
White Pawn on e2. Black Pawn on d3 and f3.
```

From e2, the White Pawn can move to e3 or e4. It can capture the Black Pawn on d3 or f3. It **cannot** capture straight to e3 if a piece is there.

**Key teaching point:** The Pawn is the only piece that captures differently from how it moves. This is its special "personality" — it marches straight but attacks at an angle.

### Step 2: Guided Practice — Pawn Parade (15-20 min)

Students pair up with boards. One player is White, one is Black. Set up only the Pawns in starting positions.

**Activity:** Each side takes turns moving one Pawn. The goal is to advance your Pawns as far as possible while capturing any Pawns that move diagonally into your path.

```
Starting position:
White Pawns: a2 b2 c2 d2 e2 f2 g2 h2
Black Pawns: a7 b7 c7 d7 e7 f7 g7 h7
```

- White moves first.
- Players must call out the square they are moving to using algebraic notation (e.g., "e4").
- If a capture is made, the player must name both squares (e.g., "exd5").

[Checklist for students:]
- [ ] I can set up the board with the light square on my right.
- [ ] I can name any square using a letter and a number.
- [ ] I move my Pawn one or two squares forward from the starting rank.
- [ ] I capture diagonally, not straight ahead.
- [ ] I call out my moves using algebraic notation.

**Teacher circulates** and corrects notation errors. Praise students who remember the diagonal capture rule.

### Step 3: Independent Practice — Pawn Race Challenge (10-15 min)

**Challenge:** On your own board, place a White Pawn on a2 and a Black Pawn on b7. Both Pawns try to reach the other side (White to a8, Black to b1). They can capture each other if they land on diagonal squares. What happens? Can the White Pawn ever capture the Black Pawn? Can they both promote?

||| Position / Criterion | Success Metric |
|||------------------------|------------------|
|| Student predicts outcome correctly | Understands that opposite-colored Pawns on adjacent files can block or capture each other |
|| Student plays 10 moves without illegal moves | Demonstrates correct Pawn movement rules |
|| Student explains why a Pawn cannot move backward | Understands Pawns only move forward |

[Student checklist:]
- [ ] I set up the challenge position correctly.
- [ ] I moved only forward, never backward.
- [ ] I tried to reach the other side (promotion rank).
- [ ] I wrote down at least 5 moves in notation.

### Step 4: Sharing & Feedback (5-10 min)

Learners present their Pawn Race results. Use the critique framework:
- **What's working:** "You remembered that Pawns capture diagonally — that's the trickiest rule!"
- **One thing to try:** "Try placing your Pawns on the same file instead of adjacent files. What changes?"
- **Question:** "If a Pawn could move backward, how would the game be different?"

## Extended Activity

**Pawn Diary:** At home, students draw a chessboard on paper and label every square. They then write a 5-move Pawn-only game in notation and bring it to the next lesson.

**Physical Activity:** Play "Body Chess" — students stand on a grid taped to the floor and move as Pawns (one step forward, diagonal capture).

**Digital Activity:** Complete the "Pawn" level on lichess.org/learn.

**Teaching Activity:** Teach a parent or sibling how the Pawn moves and how to name squares.

## Differentiation

||| For learners who need more support | For advanced learners |
||-------------------------------------|----------------------|
|| Use colored tape to mark the files and ranks on the board | Challenge them to play a full Pawn-only game and record all moves |
|| Practice naming just the corners and center squares first | Introduce the concept of Pawn chains (protecting Pawns with other Pawns) |
|| Pair with a peer who grasps notation quickly | Ask them to set up a position from notation without looking at a diagram |
