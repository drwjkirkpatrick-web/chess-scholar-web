## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. **Compose** an original chess puzzle with a clear tactical theme.
2. **Validate** that your puzzle has exactly one solution.
3. **Value** creativity — understanding that creating puzzles deepens understanding more than solving them.

## Key Inquiry Question

> How do you create a puzzle that is challenging but fair — and why is composing harder than solving?

## Chess Persona: The Queen

**Piece:** Queen  
**Value:** 9 points  
**Movement:** Any number of squares horizontally, vertically, or diagonally  
**Persona Trait:** Creativity & Composition  
**Why this piece?** The Queen is the most powerful piece because she combines the powers of Rook and Bishop. In composition, the solver must see the board as the composer sees it — with every line, every trick, and every aesthetic possibility. The Queen teaches that creating beauty requires more vision than consuming it. To compose is to become the architect of chess, not just the visitor.  
**Historical Note:** The art of chess composition dates back to the 9th-century Arab world, where puzzles were called *mansubat*. The modern era of composition began in the 19th century with the "Bohemian School," which emphasized economy and aesthetic purity. Sam Loyd (1841–1911), America's greatest puzzle composer, created thousands of problems — including the famous "Excelsior" problem where a Pawn promotes and delivers mate. Richard Réti's 1921 study (King + Pawn vs King + Pawn) is one of the most famous endgame studies ever composed, proving that even in the simplest material, art exists.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5-10 min)

**Hook:** Show the famous "Excelsior" problem by Sam Loyd (mate in 5, White to move). Ask: "What makes this a work of art, not just a puzzle?"

```
Loyd's Excelsior (simplified idea):
White: Ke1, Qh5, pawns a2,b2,c2,d4,e2,f2,g2,h2
Black: Ke8, Qe7, pawns a7,b7,c7,d7,e7,f7,g7,h7
Theme: A pawn advances from its starting square to promotion and delivers checkmate.
```

- *Why is creating a puzzle harder than solving one?* — *A solver has one path to find; a composer must foresee every wrong path and block it.*
- *What makes a puzzle "fair"?* — *It has exactly one solution, no cooked (unintended) solutions, and the solution is elegant.*
- *If you could create any puzzle, what theme would you choose?* — *[Student response]*

Explain: *Today you become a composer. You will create an original chess puzzle, test it, refine it, and present it. The Queen is our guide because she sees every possibility — and a composer must see them too.*

### Step 1: Concept Introduction — The Anatomy of a Puzzle (15-20 min)

Teach the elements of a good chess composition:

1. **Clear Theme:** Every puzzle should teach ONE idea — a fork, a pin, a decoy, a back-rank mate.
2. **Economy:** Use the fewest pieces necessary. Extra pieces distract.
3. **Uniqueness:** There should be exactly one correct solution (or one "main line" for studies).
4. **Aesthetic Value:** The solution should feel surprising but inevitable — "I should have seen that."

Teach the difference between:
- **Direct mate problem:** White to move and mate in N. The composer stipulates the number of moves.
- **Study:** White to play and win (or draw). The solution is a sequence leading to a decisive endgame.
- **Tactical puzzle:** Find the winning combination (material or mate).

**The Composer's Method:**
1. **Start with the idea:** "I want a fork that wins the Queen."
2. **Build the key position:** Place the pieces so the fork works.
3. **Add distractors:** Pieces that look important but aren't.
4. **Test for cooks:** Check if there's an unintended easier solution.
5. **Refine:** Remove unnecessary pieces; ensure the solution is forced.

Example composition process:

```
Theme: Back-rank mate with a Queen sacrifice decoy.

Step 1 — Key idea: Black's King is on g8, back rank guarded by Rook on f8.
Step 2 — Key position:
White: Qd1, Rf1, Bc4, pawns a2,b2,c2,d2,e4,f2,g2,h2
Black: Kg8, Rf8, Qd8, pawns a7,b7,c7,d7,e7,f7,g7,h7
Step 3 — Solution: 1.Qxf8+! Kxf8 2.Rxf8#
Step 4 — Test: Is there another mate? 1.Rxf8+ also wins but is not a decoy. Refine: Move Rook to e1 so 1.Rxf8+ doesn't work immediately.
```

**Key teaching point:** *A puzzle is like a lock. The composer must design a lock that opens with one key and resists every other key. If it opens with two keys, it's broken — it's "cooked."*

### Step 2: Guided Practice — Compose Together (15-20 min)

Class composes one puzzle together:

- Choose a theme (e.g., skewer, deflection, or clearance sacrifice)
- Place the target pieces on the board
- Add the "decoy" or "tactical shot" piece
- Add distractors
- Test by having volunteers try to "cook" it (find unintended solutions)
- Refine until only the intended solution works

[Checklist for students:]
- [ ] I stated the theme of our puzzle in one sentence
- [ ] I placed the minimum number of pieces needed
- [ ] I identified at least one "cook" (unintended solution) during testing
- [ ] I helped refine the position to eliminate the cook

### Step 3: Independent Practice — Original Composition (10-15 min)

**Challenge:** Each student composes an original puzzle:
- Must be a mate-in-2 or a winning tactic (material gain)
- Must use the piece persona in the title or description
- Must be tested by at least one peer
- Must be "cook-free" (peer cannot find unintended solution)

||| Position / Criterion | Success Metric |
|||------------------------|------------------|
|| Theme is clear | Peer can state theme after solving |
|| Cook-free | Peer cannot find unintended solution in 3 minutes |
|| Aesthetic value | Solution feels surprising but logical |

[Student checklist:]
- [ ] I started with a clear tactical idea, not random pieces
- [ ] I tested my puzzle with a peer and eliminated unintended solutions
- [ ] I removed all unnecessary pieces
- [ ] I gave my puzzle a creative title inspired by the piece persona

### Step 4: Sharing & Feedback (5-10 min)

Students present their puzzles on the demonstration board. The class attempts to solve them.

Use the critique framework:
- **What's working:** "Your decoy was beautiful — the Knight on f6 looked like a defender but was actually a target. That misdirection is exactly what makes a puzzle memorable."
- **One thing to try:** "Consider removing the Bishop on c1 — it doesn't participate in the solution and might confuse the solver."
- **Question:** "If someone solves your puzzle in 2 seconds, is it too easy? If they never solve it, is it too hard? What is the 'Goldilocks zone' for difficulty?"

## Assessment

||| Criteria | BE (Below Expectation) | AE (Approaching) | ME (Meets) | EE (Exceeds) |
|||----------|------------------------|-------------------|------------|--------------|
||| Theme clarity | No clear theme | Theme exists but is muddled | Clear theme; solvable | Theme is both clear AND elegantly hidden |
||| Cook-free status | Multiple unintended solutions | One cook remains after testing | Cook-free after peer testing | Cook-free AND requires multi-move calculation |
||| Composition economy | Too many unnecessary pieces | Some unnecessary pieces | Minimal, necessary pieces only | Every piece serves dual purpose (defense + misdirection) |

## Extended Activity

**The Chess Scholar Puzzle Collection:** Over the next two weeks, compose three original puzzles: one mate-in-2, one winning tactic, and one study (endgame technique). Include a title, the piece persona, and a "coach's note" explaining the lesson. Submit the collection to be printed as a booklet for younger students.

## Differentiation

||| For learners who need more support | For advanced learners |
|||-------------------------------------|----------------------|
||| Compose a mate-in-1 or simple fork | Compose a study (e.g., Bishop + wrong-color rook pawn draw) |
||| Use a provided "puzzle template" with blank pieces | Compose a helpmate (both sides cooperate to achieve mate) |
||| Work in a pair with one person placing pieces and one testing | Create a puzzle series where the same position is seen from different phases |
