## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. **Choose** 2-3 openings for White and 2-3 defenses for Black based on your style.
2. **Explain** the difference between a narrow repertoire and a broad one.
3. **Value** self-knowledge — understanding that your openings should match your personality.

## Key Inquiry Question

> How do you choose the openings that fit your style — and why is a small, deep repertoire better than a large, shallow one?

## Chess Persona: The King

**Piece:** ♔ King
**Value:** Infinite
**Movement:** One square in any direction
**Persona Trait:** Your Personal Toolbox
**Why this piece?** The King is the most important piece, and every opening decision ultimately serves the King's safety. Building a repertoire is about choosing paths that lead to positions where your King — and your style — can thrive. The King teaches us that preparation is not about complexity but about knowing where you are going.
**Historical Note:** Throughout chess history, the greatest players have been defined by their repertoires. Capablanca's Caro-Kann, Fischer's Sicilian, Karpov's English, and Kasparov's Najdorf are not just openings — they are identities.

---

## Opening Identity

### Origin Story
The concept of a "repertoire" emerged in the 19th century when chess became more competitive and players began preparing specific openings for specific opponents. Before then, players simply played what they knew. The Soviet chess school formalized repertoire building, teaching that a player should have:
- 1-2 primary openings for White
- 2-3 primary defenses for Black (one against 1.e4, one against 1.d4, and one against flank openings)
- Backup systems for surprise situations

Today, with databases and engines, repertoire building is both easier and harder — there is more information, but also more pressure to specialize.

### Famous Practitioners
- **José Raúl Capablanca:** Played the same narrow repertoire for decades, reaching depths of understanding that made his positions seem effortless.
- **Bobby Fischer:** Had a relatively narrow but brutally effective repertoire: 1.e4 as White, Sicilian and Nimzo-Indian as Black.
- **Anatoly Karpov:** Expanded his repertoire throughout his career, adding the English and Catalan to his classical systems.
- **Magnus Carlsen:** Has the broadest repertoire of any World Champion, playing virtually everything depending on the situation.

### Key Position Diagram (Repertoire Map)
A complete repertoire is not a single position but a map of positions. Here is a sample repertoire built from Volume 2 lessons:

**As White:**
- 1.e4 → Italian Game (Lesson 52) or Ruy Lopez (Lesson 56)
- 1.d4 → London System (Lesson 83) or Queen's Gambit (Lesson 79)

## Core Ideas

### Strategic Goal
**Your goal:** Build a repertoire that reflects your strengths and covers your weaknesses. If you are a tactical player, choose sharp openings (Sicilian Dragon, Fried Liver, Benko Gambit). If you are a positional player, choose solid systems (Caro-Kann, Queen's Indian, English). If you are a universal player, build a broad repertoire with one system in each category.

### Typical Middlegame Plans (by Style)
- **Aggressive players:** Sicilian Najdorf (Lesson 62), Dragon (Lesson 63), Fried Liver (Lesson 55), Benko Gambit (Lesson 92), Pirc (Lesson 74)
- **Positional players:** Caro-Kann (Lesson 73), Queen's Indian (Lesson 89), English (Lesson 93), Réti (Lesson 94), Slav (Lesson 81)
- **Universal players:** Italian Game (Lesson 52), Ruy Lopez (Lesson 56), King's Indian (Lesson 86), Queen's Gambit (Lesson 79)
- **Tricky players:** Alekhine's Defense (Lesson 76), Modern Defense (Lesson 75), Nimzo-Larsen (Lesson 96), rare defenses (Lesson 78)

### Common Endgames
Your repertoire choice determines the endgames you reach. Sicilian players often reach rook endgames with pawn majorities; Caro-Kann players reach minor-piece endgames; King's Indian players often reach middlegame attacks before the endgame. Choose openings whose endgames you enjoy playing.

## Critical Lines

### Main Line: The Narrow Repertoire
A narrow repertoire means 1-2 systems per color, studied deeply:
- **White:** 1.e4 → Italian Game (Lesson 52) against everything
- **Black:** Sicilian Najdorf (Lesson 62) against 1.e4; King's Indian (Lesson 86) against 1.d4

**Pros:** Deep knowledge, confidence, less memorization.
**Cons:** Predictable, vulnerable to specific preparation.

### Deviation / Sideline: The Broad Repertoire
A broad repertoire means 3-4 systems per color, studied moderately:
- **White:** 1.e4, 1.d4, 1.c4, 1.Nf3 — different systems depending on the opponent
- **Black:** Multiple defenses against each first move

**Pros:** Unpredictable, flexible, can choose based on opponent.
**Cons:** Shallow knowledge, more memorization, potential confusion.

### Common Trap: The Jack of All Trades
Many players fall into the trap of learning a little bit of every opening and mastering none. The result is that they know the first 3-4 moves of twenty openings but cannot play any of them well. The solution is to choose 2-3 systems and study them deeply before expanding.

## Practice Drills

### Drill 1: Memorization (5 min)
Write down your current "repertoire" — the openings you play most often as White and Black. For each, write the first 4 moves from memory.

### Drill 2: Recognition (5 min)
Identify which style each of the following openings suits best:
- Sicilian Dragon (Lesson 63): Aggressive / Positional / Universal? (*Aggressive*)
- Caro-Kann (Lesson 73): Aggressive / Positional / Universal? (*Positional*)
- London System (Lesson 83): Aggressive / Positional / Universal? (*Universal*)
- Benko Gambit (Lesson 92): Aggressive / Positional / Universal? (*Aggressive*)

### Drill 3: Decision-Making (10 min)
You are building your repertoire. You love tactical positions but often lose in complicated middlegames. Should you choose the Sicilian Najdorf (sharp but theoretical) or the Caro-Kann (solid but less tactical)? Explain how your choice reflects your strengths and weaknesses.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5–10 min)

**Hook:** Ask students:
- "What is your favorite opening — and why?" (*Discussion*)
- "Do you play the same opening every game, or do you change?" (*Discussion*)
- "Would you rather be a specialist or a generalist?" (*Discussion*)

Explain: *Today we build your personal opening repertoire — the toolkit you'll take into every tournament. It's not about copying grandmasters; it's about finding what works for YOU.*

### Step 1: Concept Introduction — The Repertoire Philosophy (15–20 min)

Define a repertoire: the set of openings you play regularly and know well. Explain the two approaches:
1. **Narrow and deep:** 2-3 systems, mastered thoroughly.
2. **Broad and shallow:** Many systems, studied moderately.

Show how famous players chose:
- Fischer: Narrow (1.e4, Sicilian, Nimzo-Indian)
- Karpov: Started narrow, expanded later
- Carlsen: Broad (plays everything)

Discuss which approach suits which personality:
- Narrow: Students who like routine and deep understanding.
- Broad: Students who like variety and adaptability.

Connect to earlier lessons by grouping openings by style (see Core Ideas).

**Key teaching point:** Your repertoire is not a prison — it is a foundation. Start small, build confidence, then expand.

### Step 2: Guided Practice — Repertoire Building (15–20 min)

Students use a template to build their repertoire:

**Template:**
- As White, my primary opening is: _____________ (e.g., Lesson 52 — Italian Game)
- As White, my backup opening is: _____________ (e.g., Lesson 93 — English)
- Against 1.e4, my primary defense is: _____________ (e.g., Lesson 61 — Sicilian)
- Against 1.e4, my backup defense is: _____________ (e.g., Lesson 73 — Caro-Kann)
- Against 1.d4, my primary defense is: _____________ (e.g., Lesson 86 — King's Indian)
- Against 1.d4, my backup defense is: _____________ (e.g., Lesson 89 — Queen's Indian)
- Against 1.c4/1.Nf3, I play: _____________ (e.g., Lesson 86 — King's Indian setups)

[Checklist for students:]
- [ ] I chose openings that match my style
- [ ] I have a primary and backup for each situation
- [ ] I can write the first 4 moves of each system from memory

Discuss: Students share their choices and explain why they suit their style.

### Step 3: Independent Practice — Repertoire Testing (10–15 min)

**Challenge:** Students play a 10-minute game using ONLY their chosen repertoire openings. After the game, they answer:
1. Did I reach my chosen opening?
2. Did I feel comfortable in the resulting position?
3. What would I change about my repertoire choice?

||| Position / Criterion | Success Metric |
|||------------------------|------------------|
||| Reached chosen opening from memory | Repertoire knowledge |
||| Felt comfortable in the middlegame | Style match |
||| Identified one strength and one weakness | Self-awareness |

[Student checklist:]
- [ ] I reached my chosen opening
- [ ] I understood the middlegame plan
- [ ] I identified whether my choice suited me

### Step 4: Sharing & Feedback (5–10 min)

Learners present their repertoire choices and game experiences. Use the critique framework:
- **What's working:** "Your choice of the Caro-Kann as a backup defense shows you value reliability, which is a smart foundation."
- **One thing to try:** "Consider adding one sharp backup system — if you only play solid openings, you may struggle in positions that require counterattacking."
- **Question:** "If your opponent knows you play the Sicilian and prepares specifically for it, what is your backup plan? Do you have a second defense ready?"

## Extended Activity

**Repertoire Deepening Project:** Choose ONE opening from your repertoire and study it for 30 minutes using online resources. Write a one-page summary including:
1. The first 8 moves of the main line.
2. One deviation or sideline you might face.
3. Your typical middlegame plan.
4. One famous game in this opening.
