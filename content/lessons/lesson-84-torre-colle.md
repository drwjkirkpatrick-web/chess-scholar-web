## Chess Persona: The Bishop

**Piece:** ♗ Bishop  
**Value:** 3 points  
**Movement:** Diagonally any number of squares  
**Persona Trait:** Quiet Development  
**Why this piece?** Both the Torre and Colle systems develop the bishop to d3 or g5 quietly, almost apologetically — then unleash it in a surprise attack. Like the Bishop gliding along a diagonal, these systems appear harmless until the diagonal opens and the attack crashes through.  
**Historical Note:** The Colle System is named after Belgian master Edgard Colle (1897–1932), who won many brilliant games with the d3/e3/Nbd2 setup. The Torre Attack is named after Mexican master Carlos Torre (1905–1978), who used Bg5 to pin the knight and create tactical opportunities.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5-10 min)

**Hook:** Show two positions side by side: the Torre (1.d4 d5 2.Nf3 e6 3.Bg5) and the Colle (1.d4 d5 2.e3 Nf6 3.Bd3). Ask: "Both openings look like White is barely trying to win. Is White asleep? Or is there a hidden trap?"

- *Why does White play so slowly?* — To build a solid position and strike when Black relaxes.
- *What is the common idea in both systems?* — The e3-e4 pawn break, opening lines for the bishop on d3.
- *Which is more aggressive, Torre or Colle?* — The Torre is slightly more aggressive with Bg5; the Colle is more purely positional.

Explain: *Today we'll study two "quiet" openings that are secretly dangerous — the Torre and Colle — using the Bishop as our guide.*

### Step 1: Opening Identity — The Torre and Colle Story (15 min)

**Origin Story:**
The Colle System was developed by Edgard Colle in the 1920s as a way for club players to get playable positions without memorizing theory. The idea: play d4, e3, Nf3, Bd3, Nbd2, then e4. The Torre Attack, associated with Carlos Torre, uses Bg5 instead of Bd3, pinning the knight and creating different types of pressure. Both systems are "anti-theory" weapons that rely on understanding plans rather than memorizing moves.

**Famous Practitioners:**
- **Edgard Colle** — the namesake; won brilliant attacking games with the Colle-Zukertort system
- **Carlos Torre** — defeated Emanuel Lasker with the Torre Attack in 1925
- **Pia Cramling** — modern grandmaster who has used the Colle to defeat elite opponents
- **Artur Yusupov** — played the Torre as a solid, reliable system in his teaching games

**Key Position Diagram — The Colle Setup:**
After 1.d4 d5 2.e3 Nf6 3.Bd3 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nd2 e6 6.f4, White prepares the e4 break. The bishop on d3 eyes the h7-pawn, and the knight on d2 supports e4.

**The Torre Setup:**
After 1.d4 d5 2.Nf3 e6 3.Bg5 Nf6 4.Nbd2 Be7 5.e3 c5 6.c3 Nc6 7.Bd3, White has the bishop pin on g5 and prepares either e4 or a Ne5/Bxf6 plan.

### Step 2: Core Ideas — Strategic Goals for Both Sides (10 min)

**Strategic Goal:**
- **White (Colle):** Build a solid center with e3, develop pieces harmoniously, and time the e4 break perfectly. After e4, the bishop on d3 becomes a monster on the h7-b1 diagonal.
- **White (Torre):** Pin the f6-knight with Bg5, making it harder for Black to challenge the center. Aim for Ne5 or Bxf6 to create doubled pawns and weak squares.
- **Black:** Prevent e4 with ...e6/...Nbd7/...c5. If White plays e4 anyway, capture and open the center for piece activity.

**Typical Middlegame Plans:**
- **Colle:** e4, then e5 (if Black plays ...dxe4, recapture with Nxe4 and attack). Sometimes White castles queenside and launches a kingside pawn storm.
- **Torre:** Bxf6 (doubling Black's pawns), then Ne5 with pressure on f7 or d7. The h4-g4 pawn storm is also common.

**Common Endgames:**
- Both systems tend toward quiet, balanced endgames if Black neutralizes the attack.
- Torre endgames often feature bishop vs. knight with White having better pawn structure.
- Colle endgames may feature White's isolated d-pawn after e4/dxe4, which is both a strength and a weakness.

### Step 3: Critical Lines — Main Line, Deviation, and Trap (10 min)

**Main Line — Colle-Zukertort:**
```
1.d4 d5 2.e3 Nf6 3.Bd3 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nd2 e6 6.Ngf3 Bd6 7.O-O O-O 8.Re1 Qc7 9.e4
```
White has built the classic Colle structure and now strikes in the center. Black must respond precisely.

**Deviation — The Torre vs. King's Indian Setup:**
```
1.d4 Nf6 2.Nf3 g6 3.Bg5 Bg7 4.Nbd2 O-O 5.e4 d6 6.Be2 h6 7.Bh4 Nbd7 8.O-O e5
```
The Torre can be played against almost anything, including the King's Indian. White's setup is always solid.

**Common Trap — The Colle e4 Trap:**
```
1.d4 d5 2.e3 Nf6 3.Bd3 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nd2 e5?? 6.dxe5 Nxe5 7.Bb5+
```
Black plays ...e5 too early, thinking they've equalized. But 7.Bb5+ wins the knight on e5 after the king moves. Moral: never play ...e5 in the Colle without checking Bb5+.

### Step 5: Practice Drills (10 min)

**Drill 1 — Memorization:**
Set up the Colle System position from memory after 1.d4 d5 2.e3 Nf6 3.Bd3 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nd2 e6 6.f4. Then set up the Torre after 1.d4 d5 2.Nf3 e6 3.Bg5 Nf6 4.Nbd2 Be7 5.e3 c5 6.c3.

**Drill 2 — Recognition:**
Show positions from the Colle, Torre, and London. Ask students to identify which is which based on bishop placement (Bd3 vs. Bg5 vs. Bf4).

**Drill 3 — Decision-making:**
In the Colle, after 1.d4 d5 2.e3 Nf6 3.Bd3 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nd2 e6, White has a choice: 6.f4 (Stonewall-like) or 6.Ngf3 (pure Colle). Which leads to a faster e4 break?

### Step 6: Sharing & Feedback (5 min)

- **What's working:** "You clearly understand the e3-e4 break in the Colle."
- **One thing to try:** "Look at what happens if Black plays ...Ne4 early in the Colle — how does White respond?"
- **Question:** "When would you choose Torre over Colle?"
