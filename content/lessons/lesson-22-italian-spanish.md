## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. **Play** the first 5-6 moves of the Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4) and Spanish (3.Bb5).
2. **Explain** why these openings develop pieces toward the center.
3. **Value** tradition — understanding that classic patterns have lasted centuries for a reason.

## Key Inquiry Question

> Why have the Italian and Spanish openings been played for 500 years — and what makes a classic opening stand the test of time?

## Chess Persona: The Bishop

**Piece:** Bishop  
**Value:** 3  
**Movement:** Diagonally, any number of squares  
**Persona Trait:** Vision & Classical Development  
**Why this piece?** The Bishop is the star of both the Italian Game (Bc4) and the Spanish Game (Bb5). In both openings, the Bishop slides out early to control key diagonals and eye sensitive squares — f7 in the Italian, the knight in the Spanish. The Bishop teaches us that seeing from a distance and controlling long diagonals is the essence of classical development.  
**Historical Note:** The Ruy Lopez (Spanish Game) is named after a Spanish priest, Ruy López de Segura, who analyzed it in his 1561 book *Libro de la invencion liberal y arte del juego del axedrez*. It is one of the oldest recorded chess openings and remains a staple of grandmaster play today.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5-10 min)

**Hook:** Show the position after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 and ask: "White has two good Bishop moves here — where should it go?"

- *What does Bc4 (Italian) attack?* (The f7 square — the weakest point in Black's position because it is only defended by the King.)
- *What does Bb5 (Spanish) do?* (Pins the knight to the king and puts pressure on Black's most developed piece.)
- *Which one is better?* (Neither is objectively "better" — they are different paths to the same goal: control the center and prepare to castle.)

Explain: *Today the Bishop is our guide. It will show us how two of the oldest openings in chess have survived 500 years of competition.*

### Step 1: Concept Introduction — The Italian Game (15-20 min)

The Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4) is one of the most natural ways to start a chess game. The Bishop on c4 eyes f7, the weakest square in Black's camp.

```
Italian Game — Main Line
1.e4 e5
2.Nf3 Nc6
3.Bc4 Bc5
4.c3 Nf6
5.d4 exd4
6.cxd4 Bb4+
7.Nc3 Nxe4
8.0-0 Bxc3
9.d5!
```

**Key ideas in the Italian:**
- White controls the center with pawns on d4 and e4
- The Bishop on c4 pressures f7
- White aims for rapid development and early castling
- The d4 pawn break is thematic — opening lines in the center

**Common mistake:** Playing 4.Ng5? (the Fried Liver Attack) too early. It looks aggressive but violates opening principles by moving a piece twice before development is complete.

**Key teaching point:** The Italian Game is about rapid, natural development. Every move improves the position without forcing anything.

### Step 2: Guided Practice — The Spanish Game (15-20 min)

The Spanish Game, or Ruy Lopez (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5), is more subtle. The Bishop does not attack f7 immediately; instead, it questions the knight and prepares long-term pressure.

```
Ruy Lopez — Main Line (Morphy Defense)
1.e4 e5
2.Nf3 Nc6
3.Bb5 a6
4.Ba4 Nf6
5.0-0 Be7
6.Re1 b5
7.Bb3 d6
8.c3 0-0
9.h3
```

**Key ideas in the Spanish:**
- White does not win material immediately — White wins TIME and POSITION
- After ...a6 and ...b5, Black gains space but loosens the queenside
- White's pieces prepare to occupy the center with d4
- The h3 move prevents ...Bg4, keeping the knight stable on f3

[Checklist for students:]
- [ ] I can play the first 5 moves of the Italian Game from memory
- [ ] I can play the first 6 moves of the Ruy Lopez from memory
- [ ] I can explain why Bc4 is aggressive and Bb5 is positional
- [ ] I can identify the center squares each side controls after move 6

### Step 3: Independent Practice — Opening Identification Challenge (10-15 min)

**Challenge:** Students are given 4 positions after move 6. They must identify which opening each came from (Italian, Spanish, or neither) and explain why.

||| Position / Criterion | Success Metric |
|||------------------------|------------------|
||| Position 1: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 | Italian — Bishop on c4 |
||| Position 2: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 a6 | Spanish — Bishop on b5, Black plays ...a6 |
||| Position 3: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Nc3 Nf6 | Four Knights — neither opening |
||| Position 4: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.b4 | Evans Gambit — Italian family |

[Student checklist:]
- [ ] I identified the Italian Game correctly by the Bishop on c4
- [ ] I identified the Spanish Game correctly by the Bishop on b5
- [ ] I explained why a position belongs to neither opening
- [ ] I described the strategic difference between Bc4 and Bb5

### Step 4: Sharing & Feedback (5-10 min)

Learners present their solutions or discuss positions. Use the critique framework:
- **What's working:** "Your explanation of why the Spanish is more patient than the Italian is exactly right — the Bishop on b5 is playing for long-term pressure, not immediate tricks."
- **One thing to try:** "Try comparing what happens after move 10 in both openings — which one gives White more central pawns?"
- **Question:** "If you were Black, would you rather face the Italian or the Spanish — and why?"
