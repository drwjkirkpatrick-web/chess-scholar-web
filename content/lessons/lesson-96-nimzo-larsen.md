## Chess Persona: The Bishop

**Piece:** ♗ Bishop
**Value:** 3 points
**Movement:** Diagonally any number of squares
**Persona Trait:** Fianchetto from the Flank
**Why this piece?** The Nimzo-Larsen Attack is defined by the fianchettoed bishop on b2. This bishop controls the long diagonal from a1 to h8, aiming at the enemy kingside and supporting central control. Combined with e4 or d4, the Nimzo-Larsen creates unique positions where White's queen bishop is the star.
**Historical Note:** The opening is named after Aron Nimzowitsch and Bent Larsen. Nimzowitsch pioneered the ideas in the 1920s, and Larsen popularized it as a practical weapon in the 1960s–70s, using it to defeat world champions.

---

## Opening Identity

### Origin Story
The Nimzo-Larsen Attack (1.b3) is one of the most original flank openings. Unlike 1.c4 or 1.Nf3, which control the center from the side, 1.b3 prepares to fianchetto the queen's bishop on b2 — a rarer and more surprising plan. The opening was played occasionally in the 19th century but was considered eccentric until Aron Nimzowitsch demonstrated its strategic validity as part of the hypermodern revolution. Bent Larsen, one of the strongest players of the 1960s–70s, made 1.b3 his trademark weapon, using it to defeat world champions and reach the Candidates Matches.

### Famous Practitioners
- **Aron Nimzowitsch:** The hypermodern pioneer whose ideas on flank control and prophylaxis underpin the Nimzo-Larsen's philosophy.
- **Bent Larsen:** The Danish grandmaster who popularized 1.b3 at the highest level, proving it was not just a trick but a serious weapon.
- **Artur Yusupov:** The Russian-German grandmaster has employed the Nimzo-Larsen successfully, blending classical understanding with hypermodern flair.
- **Jon Ludwig Hammer:** The Norwegian grandmaster and Magnus Carlsen's second has played the Nimzo-Larsen, keeping the tradition alive in modern chess.

### Key Position Diagram (The Nimzo-Larsen Tabiya)
After **1.b3 e5 2.Bb2 Nc6 3.e3 Nf6 4.Bb5 d5 5.d3 Be7 6.Nd2 O-O 7.Ngf3 Bd6 8.O-O Re8**, the Nimzo-Larsen tabiya is reached:

```
White: King on g1, Queen on d1, Rooks on a1/f1, Knights on d2/f3,
       Bishops on b2/b5, pawns on a2/b3/c2/d3/e3/f2/g2/h2.
Black: King on g8, Queen on d8, Rooks on a8/e8, Knights on c6/f6,
       Bishops on c8/d6, pawns on a7/b7/c7/d5/e5/f7/g7/h7.
```

**What makes this position special:** White has a fianchettoed bishop on b2 controlling the long diagonal and a bishop on b5 pinning the knight on c6. The position is unique to the Nimzo-Larsen — no other opening reaches this exact setup. White's plan includes Ne2-g3, c4, or f4 to challenge Black's center. Black has a solid classical center but must be careful not to overextend. The game is a strategic battle where White's unusual piece placement creates fresh challenges.

## Core Ideas

### Strategic Goal
**White's goal:** Control the long diagonal with the Bb2, develop flexibly, and use the unique piece placement to create original positions. White can challenge the center with c4, e4, or f4 depending on Black's setup. The Nimzo-Larsen is about creativity — reaching positions your opponent has never studied.
**Black's goal:** Occupy the center with ...e5 and ...d5, develop naturally, and prove that classical principles still apply. Black must avoid getting confused by White's unusual setup and focus on solid development.

### Typical Middlegame Plans
- **White:** Play Nf3, e3, Bb5, Ne2, and prepare c4 or f4. The bishop on b2 is a powerful piece that supports both central and kingside play. White often castles kingside and expands on the queenside with a3, b4, and c4.
- **Black:** Play ...Nc6, ...Nf6, ...d5, and ...Be7 or ...Bd6. The center is the key — if Black can maintain pawns on e5 and d5 with piece support, White's flank play may prove too slow.
- **Both sides:** The game often revolves around whether White's Bb2 and unusual development can offset Black's central space. Tactics are less common than in other openings; strategic understanding is crucial.

### Common Endgames
Nimzo-Larsen endgames often feature bishop-vs-knight imbalances because of the unusual piece placement. The bishop on b2 remains a strong piece in open positions. If White succeeds in exchanging dark-squared bishops, the b2-bishop becomes even more powerful. Rook endgames are usually balanced if Black maintained the center.

## Critical Lines

### Main Line: Classical Setup (8 moves)
```
1.b3 e5 2.Bb2 Nc6 3.e3 Nf6 4.Bb5 d5 5.d3 Be7 6.Nd2 O-O 7.Ngf3 Bd6 8.O-O Re8
```
This is the main tabiya. White has the Bb2 and Bb5 battery; Black has a solid center. The game is a strategic battle where White's creativity is tested against Black's classical principles.

### Deviation / Sideline: The Modern Defense Response
```
1.b3 e5 2.Bb2 Nc6 3.e3 Nf6 4.Bb5 d6 5.d3 g6 6.Nd2 Bg7 7.Ngf3 O-O 8.O-O Re8
```
Black plays ...g6 and ...Bg7, creating a Modern Defense setup against White's flank system. This leads to unusual positions where both sides have fianchettoed bishops — a true hypermodern duel.

### Common Trap: The Overambitious Bb2
```
1.b3 e5 2.Bb2 Nc6 3.e3 Nf6 4.Bb5 Bd6 5.Na3?
```
White plays Na3, which looks like it supports c4 but actually places the knight on the rim. Black replies ...a6, attacking the bishop, and follows with ...d5, seizing the center. The knight on a3 is poorly placed and takes multiple moves to re-enter the game. Better is Nf3 or d3, maintaining solid development.

## Practice Drills

### Drill 1: Memorization (5 min)
Set up the Nimzo-Larsen from move 1: **1.b3 e5 2.Bb2 Nc6**
- Place all pieces correctly.
- Continue with 3.e3 Nf6 4.Bb5 d5 5.d3.
- Identify the two diagonals White's bishops control.

### Drill 2: Recognition (5 min)
In the tabiya after 5...Be7, identify:
- What is White's primary strategic weapon? (*Answer: The Bb2 on the long diagonal*)
- What is Black's main break? (*Answer: ...d4 or ...e4 to exploit the center*)
- Why is the Nimzo-Larsen considered "original"? (*Answer: It reaches unique positions no other opening produces*)

### Drill 3: Decision-Making (10 min)
You are White. After 1.b3 e5 2.Bb2 Nc6, should you play 3.e3 (solid) or 3.d4 (challenging the center)? Evaluate both options and explain which fits the Nimzo-Larsen's philosophy better.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5–10 min)

**Hook:** Ask students:
- "What if your first move didn't control the center at all?" (*1.b3 doesn't control e4, d4, or d5 directly*)
- "Why would a grandmaster play a move that seems to do nothing?" (*It prepares Bb2, a powerful long-range bishop*)
- "Is chess an art or a science?" (*The Nimzo-Larsen is for artists*)

Explain: *Today we study the Nimzo-Larsen Attack — the opening for creative players who want to avoid theory and reach unique positions. It's not for everyone, but in the right hands, it's deadly.*

### Step 1: Concept Introduction — The Flank Fianchetto (15–20 min)

Show 1.b3 on the board. Ask: what does this move do? It prepares Bb2, which controls the a1-h8 diagonal. Compare:
- King's fianchetto (g3, Bg2): controls the a1-h8 diagonal from the kingside
- Queen's fianchetto (b3, Bb2): controls the a1-h8 diagonal from the queenside
- The b3 fianchetto is rarer because the queen's bishop usually develops to e2 or d3

Explain that the Nimzo-Larsen is about originality. While 1.e4 and 1.d4 lead to heavily analyzed positions, 1.b3 often leads to positions your opponent has never seen. This can be an advantage if you understand the ideas better than they do.

**Key teaching point:** The Nimzo-Larsen proves that development and piece activity matter more than immediate center occupation. The Bb2 is a powerful piece that influences the whole board.

### Step 2: Guided Practice — The Nimzo-Larsen Tabiya (15–20 min)

Walk through the main line together. Students follow along:

```
1.b3 e5 2.Bb2 Nc6 3.e3 Nf6 4.Bb5 d5 5.d3 Be7 6.Nd2 O-O 7.Ngf3 Bd6 8.O-O Re8
```

[Checklist for students:]
- [ ] White has bishops on b2 and b5 — what are they doing?
- [ ] Black has a solid center — how does White challenge it?
- [ ] The knight on d2 supports c4 or e4 — which is better?
- [ ] Compare this to Lesson 93 (English Opening) — how is it different?

Discuss: White should consider c4 to challenge d5, or Ne2-g3 to support kingside play. Black should maintain the center and look for ...a6 to chase the Bb5, followed by ...d4.

### Step 3: Independent Practice — Nimzo-Larsen Creativity (10–15 min)

**Challenge:** In pairs, students play from 1.b3. White must reach a Nimzo-Larsen setup (Bb2, e3/d3, Nf3, O-O). Black can play any classical response. Play 10 moves and evaluate whether White's unusual setup created any real advantages.

||| Position / Criterion | Success Metric |
|||------------------------|------------------|
||| White plays Bb2 within 2 moves | Correct Nimzo-Larsen setup |
||| White develops at least 3 pieces before moving central pawns | Patient development |
||| Either side reaches a recognizable Nimzo-Larsen tabiya | Opening understanding |

[Student checklist:]
- [ ] I understand why 1.b3 prepares a strong bishop on b2
- [ ] I can explain how the Nimzo-Larsen differs from 1.c4 and 1.Nf3
- [ ] I avoided moving the same piece twice in the opening

### Step 4: Sharing & Feedback (5–10 min)

Learners present their games. Use the critique framework:
- **What's working:** "Your decision to keep the bishop on b2 and support it with c4 showed excellent understanding of the Nimzo-Larsen's long-term strategy."
- **One thing to try:** "After Black plays ...d5, consider Bb5+ to disrupt development before committing to c4 or e4."
- **Question:** "If Black plays ...d5 and ...e5, should you challenge the center immediately or develop more pieces first? What does the hypermodern school say?"
