# Lesson Plan 05: The King

> *The most important piece; the object of the game.*

---

**Module Reference:** Prompt 05 — lesson-05-the-king.md  
**Duration:** 60 min  
**Age Group:** 8-10  
**Chess Skill Level:** ♙ Pawn Tier  
**Tool:** Physical board (primarily)

---

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. **Move** the King correctly — one square in any direction.
2. **Explain** why the King is the most important piece even though it moves slowly.
3. **Value** protection — understanding that the most valuable things often need the most care.

## Key Inquiry Question

> Why is the slowest-moving piece also the most important — and what does that teach us about leadership?

## Chess Persona: The King

**Piece:** King  
**Value:** Infinite points (the game ends when the King is checkmated)  
**Movement:** One square in any direction — horizontally, vertically, or diagonally  
**Persona Trait:** Importance & Vulnerability  
**Why this piece?** The King is the ultimate paradox: it is the most important piece (the entire game revolves around protecting it), yet it moves only one square at a time. This teaches students that value and power are not the same thing. The King cannot charge into battle like the Queen or sweep across the board like the Rook, but without the King, the game is lost. In life, leaders are often not the fastest or loudest — they are the ones everyone depends on.  
**Historical Note:** The King has always been the central figure in chess. In the 6th-century Indian game chaturanga, the "raja" (king) moved exactly one square in any direction — the same as today. The Persian word "shah" (king) is where we get the word "chess" and the cry "check!" (from "shah mat," meaning "the king is helpless" or "the king is dead"). For over 1,400 years, the King has remained the heart of the game, even as other pieces gained new powers around him.

## Lesson Development

### Introduction (5-10 min)

**Hook:** Place the White King on e1. Ask students to place their finger on every square the King could move to.

- "Why can the King only move one square when the Queen can move across the whole board?" *Because the King is not a warrior — he is the leader who must be protected.*
- "What happens to the game if the King is captured?" *The game is over — that player loses.*
- "Can the King ever move into danger?" *No — the King can never move into check (where an enemy piece could capture him).*

Explain: *Today we meet the King — the slowest piece, but the most important. The entire game of chess is about keeping the King safe. He teaches us that the most valuable things need the most protection.*

### Step 1: Concept Introduction — King Movement & Safety (15-20 min)

**Teach King movement:**
- The King moves **exactly one square** in any direction: horizontally, vertically, or diagonally.
- The King **cannot move into check** — a square attacked by an enemy piece is off-limits.
- The King **can capture** an enemy piece if it is adjacent and the capture does not expose the King to check.
- The King **cannot be captured** — the game ends when the King is in checkmate (trapped and attacked with no escape).

**Demonstration position:**

```
White King on e1.
Black Queen on e8 (same file, nothing blocking).
```

The Black Queen attacks e1, so the White King is in **check**. The King cannot stay on e1. Where can he go?
- d1? *Yes, if not attacked.*
- d2? *Yes, if not attacked.*
- e2? *No — the Queen still attacks e2 along the e-file.*
- f1? *Yes, if not attacked.*
- f2? *Yes, if not attacked.*

**Key teaching point:** The King is limited not just by his one-square movement, but by the rule that he can never enter check. This makes him the most carefully guarded piece on the board.

**Special King rule — King vs. King:**

```
White King on e4.
Black King on e6.
```

The Kings can never stand next to each other. Why? Because if they did, both would be attacking each other, and neither could move into check. The rule that Kings cannot be adjacent is built into the game.

**Key teaching point:** The King is both the goal of the game (attack the enemy King) and the piece that needs the most protection. Every other piece exists to serve the King.

### Step 2: Guided Practice — King Escape Drill (15-20 min)

Students work in pairs.

**Activity:** Set up positions where a King is in check. Students must find a safe square for the King to move to. If no safe square exists, the King is in checkmate (we will learn that fully in Lesson 9 — for now, just identify that there is no escape).

```
Drill Position 1:
White King on e1.
Black Rook on e8.
Safe squares: d1, d2, f1, f2 (assuming no other Black pieces).

Drill Position 2:
White King on e1.
Black Queen on e8.
Black Bishop on c3.
Safe squares: d2 only! (d1 is attacked by the Bishop on c3; f1 and f2 are attacked by the Queen; e2 is attacked by the Queen).
```

**Important:** For this lesson, students only need to identify safe squares. The full "three ways out of check" (move, block, capture) comes in Lesson 9.

[Checklist for students:]
- [ ] I can move the King one square in any direction.
- [ ] I check if the destination square is safe (not attacked by any enemy piece).
- [ ] I remember that the King cannot move into check.
- [ ] I can identify when the King has no safe squares.
- [ ] I explain why the King is more important than the Queen.

### Step 3: Independent Practice — The King's Journey (10-15 min)

**Challenge:** Set up an empty board with only the White King on a1 and the Black King on h8. Place Black Pawns on c3, c4, c5 and Black Rook on f1. The White King must travel from a1 to h8, but he can only step on squares that are not attacked by any Black piece. Can he make it?

```
Position:
White King on a1.
Black King on h8.
Black Pawns on c3, c4, c5.
Black Rook on f1.
```

**Hint:** The Black Rook on f1 attacks the entire first rank (a1-h1). The White King cannot stay on rank 1! He must move up to rank 2. But the Pawns on c3, c4, c5 control squares diagonally in front of them. The b-Pawn on c3 controls b2 and d2.

||| Position / Criterion | Success Metric |
|||------------------------|------------------|
|| Student finds a valid path for the King | Understands safe vs unsafe squares |
|| Student identifies squares controlled by Pawns | Understands Pawn diagonal capture |
|| Student explains why the King cannot rush straight across | Understands that the slowest piece needs the most planning |

[Student checklist:]
- [ ] I set up the challenge position correctly.
- [ ] I checked every potential square for safety before moving the King.
- [ ] I found at least one valid path.
- [ ] I identified squares attacked by the Rook and Pawns.
- [ ] I explained why the King cannot move into check.

### Step 4: Sharing & Feedback (5-10 min)

Learners present their King's Journey paths. Use the critique framework:
- **What's working:** "You carefully checked every square before moving — that is exactly how you protect a King!"
- **One thing to try:** "What if there was also a Black Bishop on a6? Would your path still work?"
- **Question:** "In a real game, who protects the King? Is it the King's job to run, or the other pieces' job to defend him?"

## Extended Activity

**King's Journal:** Students draw a 5×5 mini-board and place a King in the center. They shade all squares the King can reach and label them. They repeat with the King in a corner and compare.

**Physical Activity:** "Protect the King" — one student is the King (wearing a crown) and must move one step at a time. Other students are "pieces" who must physically block imaginary attackers.

**Digital Activity:** Complete King movement puzzles on lichess.org/learn.

**Teaching Activity:** Teach a family member why the King is more important than the Queen even though it moves slower.
